Section 1

Problem Statement

School students in the Class 6 to Class 9 age band are generally expected to develop some familiarity with computers, yet the way programming is first introduced makes it appear difficult, abstract and reserved for a small group of students who already perform well in mathematics. The problem is not that the students lack ability. It is that the teaching method attaches syntax to a concept the student has not yet experienced.

A typical first lesson asks a beginner to type an instruction like print("Hello") and accept a rule about brackets and quotation marks before they know what a program is or why order matters. Errors appear as messages written for an experienced programmer. Confusion is read as inability. Very quickly the student concludes that coding is "not for them", and that conclusion is usually permanent.

Precise statement of the problem

Beginner-level programming is commonly taught syntax-first, which creates an avoidable barrier: students abandon the subject because they meet the notation of coding before its logic, and because mistakes are presented as failures rather than as information.

Manifestations observed in the school context

  • Fear of the error message. Students stop experimenting as soon as an error appears, because nothing has taught them that errors are normal and informative.
  • Loss of sequence reasoning. Students who can describe steps out loud still cannot place them in a correct order when the steps are written down, because nothing has made the order logically necessary.
  • No distinction between ideas. Repetition, decision-making and storage are all seen as "computers doing things" rather than as three separate, nameable concepts.
  • Confidence gap. Students who are not already confident in mathematics withdraw from computer activities long before their actual understanding is tested.
  • One-pass teaching. The subject is taught once and not revisited, so there is no second opportunity for the student who needed more time.

Section 2

Need Assessment

Why this project is worth doing in this community, and what evidence determines its shape.

Educational need

Computational thinking — breaking a task into ordered steps, recognising repetition, and making decisions from conditions — is now a foundational skill across every field the students will enter. Introducing it during the school years is far more effective than repairing the gap later, because at this age the concepts can still be taught as concrete activities rather than as abstractions.

The current syllabus teaches computer use, but the reasoning behind programs is not addressed in a way that reaches a mixed-ability classroom.

Community need

The project addresses a school community that has enthusiasm for technology but limited access to specialised teaching resources for programming. A resource that requires no installation, no internet connection and no specialist teacher is directly usable in that setting, and it can be handed over and reused without the project team present.

The benefit therefore does not end when the visit ends — the school keeps a working teaching aid.

Assessment of the existing situation

Table 2.1 — Gap analysis: what is needed against what currently exists
What the students need What currently exists Gap this project fills
An experience of a concept before its name Definitions and syntax presented first Five activities that make the concept playable before it is named
Permission to be wrong Errors presented as failures Instant, explanation-first feedback on every answer
A second attempt at the same idea Single-pass explanation Restartable activities with no penalty and no time limit
A resource the teacher can keep using Material tied to one lesson A standalone offline application left with the school
Confidence for students who are not "good at maths" Maths ability used as a proxy for coding ability Activities that reward careful reasoning, not calculation

The need identified above was established from the syllabus requirements of Topic 14, the project team's review of common beginner difficulties in programming education, and the pre-session survey instrument contained on the Surveys page. The survey is designed to be completed by students at the start of the session and is the vehicle through which this assessment is verified against the actual classroom.

Section 3

Methodology

The approach, the session structure, and the method used to measure whether it worked.

Approach

The project uses an activity-based, concept-first method. Rather than explaining a programming idea and then demonstrating it, each concept is introduced as a game that cannot be won without using the idea. The technical term is supplied only after the student has succeeded, so that the vocabulary names an achievement instead of adding a new thing to memorise.

Session structure — one day / one night prototype

Table 3.1 — Session plan
Phase Activity Purpose Approx. time
1 · Entry Welcome, introduction of the team, explanation of what the session is Reduce nervousness; establish that nobody is being graded 10 min
2 · Baseline Pre-session survey and pre-test Establish what students already know and how they feel about coding 15 min
3 · Core The five activities, demonstrated then played in small groups Teach the five concepts through play 90 min
4 · Consolidation Recap of the five concepts by name, with students describing them in their own words Attach vocabulary to the experiences just had 15 min
5 · Measurement Post-test and post-session feedback Measure the change and collect improvement suggestions 20 min
6 · Closing Thank-you, handover of the resource, group photograph [if permitted by school] Close the visit and leave the resource usable 10 min

Total contact time ≈ 2 hours 40 minutes. Times are indicative: the Core phase should be extended if the students are engaged, and shortened only as a last resort, since it carries the actual learning.

Delivery method

👥

Small groups

Students work in groups of three or four per device. Grouping encourages the verbal reasoning that makes the concepts stick, and it works around a shortage of computers.

🧑‍🏫

Demonstrate, then hand over

Each activity is demonstrated once on a shared screen, then played by the students. The team moves between groups and asks questions rather than supplying answers.

🔁

Repeat without penalty

Every activity can be restarted freely and nothing is recorded against the student. Students are actively encouraged to try again after a wrong answer.

Measurement method

The project uses a single-group pre-test / post-test design. The same short test is administered before and after the session, covering the five concepts. Because the activities themselves record a score, a second, independent measure is available from the practical exercises. The improvements between the two tests are analysed and presented on the Dashboard page.

Honest limits of this method

A single-group design cannot fully separate the effect of the activities from the effect of simply having an enthusiastic team visit for the day, and a one-day session cannot show whether the learning lasts. These limits are stated here deliberately, and both are addressed in the Recommendations.

Section 5

Activity Plan

What each activity teaches, how it is played, and how success is recognised.

🧭

1. Algorithm Maze

Concept: Sequence · algorithms

The student builds a list of movement commands and presses Run to make a robot travel through a maze to a goal. If the robot hits a wall the sequence is wrong, and the mistake is visible immediately.

Rules: Commands are added one at a time; the list can be edited before running; the goal must be reached without touching a wall.

Scoring: three stars for the shortest possible route, two for a near-optimal route, one for reaching the goal by any valid route.

🐞

2. Find the Bug

Concept: Debugging · logical errors

A short program is displayed with a mistake somewhere in it. The student identifies which line is responsible. The programs deliberately include cases where the code runs but produces the wrong answer.

Rules: One answer per question; the explanation appears immediately after answering; the activity can be restarted at any time.

Scoring: one point per correctly identified bug, with the final score expressed as a percentage.

🔀

3. If–Else Challenge

Concept: Conditions · decision-making

Students are shown a rule and a set of values, and must predict which branch the program will follow. Situations are drawn from everyday school life — a gate that opens above a certain class, a bonus above a certain score.

Rules: Conditions are checked in order and the first true one wins; the difference between > and >= is explored directly.

Scoring: one point per correct prediction with an explanation for each.

🔍

4. Pattern Detective

Concept: Loops · repetition

Students examine a sequence of numbers or shapes, work out the rule that generates it, and state what comes next. Rules include counting up, counting down, doubling and adding the previous two terms.

Rules: The explanation rewrites the rule as a loop, connecting the puzzle to how a computer would repeat the work.

Scoring: one point per correct next term, with extra credit in discussion for students who can state the rule aloud.

🖨️

5. Guess the Output

Concept: Variables · tracing

Students follow a short program by hand, keeping track of what is stored in each named value, and predict what will be displayed. Programs include loops that change a value repeatedly.

Rules: The program is read from first line to last; students are encouraged to use paper to write down each value as it changes.

Scoring: one point per correct output, with a line-by-line explanation of the trace.

📈

Progress and scoring across all five

Each activity records its own score and marks itself complete. Completion across the set is shown as a progress indicator, so a student can see how far through the five they have come and return to a half-finished activity later.

Nothing is uploaded and no personal information is collected — progress is stored only in the browser on the device being used.

Section 6

Expected Outcomes

What the project expects to observe, and how each outcome would be recognised.

For the students

  • A measurable improvement between pre-test and post-test on the five concepts.
  • The ability to name the five concepts and describe at least three of them in their own words.
  • A visible reduction in hesitation — students attempting an activity without waiting to be told the answer is safe.
  • Students helping each other within their groups, which indicates the concepts have been understood well enough to explain.

For the school

  • A reusable offline resource that needs no installation, no internet and no specialist teacher.
  • A short set of printed instruments the teacher can run again independently.
  • A record of which concepts proved hardest for the participating students, so future teaching can spend longer on them.

For the project team

  • Practical experience of explaining technical material to a non-technical audience.
  • Direct evidence about where beginners get stuck, which is more useful than any prediction.
  • The complete set of artefacts the CEP requires, produced from a real piece of work rather than assembled afterwards.

What is not claimed

  • This project does not claim to teach programming in one session — it introduces five concepts and measures the introduction.
  • It does not claim that students will be able to write independent programs afterwards.
  • It does not claim lasting retention, which a one-day session cannot demonstrate. Testing that would require a follow-up visit, as recommended below.

Challenges Anticipated

Problems foreseen in advance, together with the mitigation planned for each.

Table 7.1 — Risk register
Challenge Why it matters Mitigation planned
Not enough computers for one per student Some students would only watch, and watching does not teach the reasoning Deliberate small-group rotation so every student operates the device in turn; the group discussion itself reinforces the concept
Power cut or device failure The core phase would be lost in the middle The site runs entirely offline from a local copy; printed backup versions of all five activities are carried to the session
Wide variation in student ability Fast students finish and lose interest; slower students feel left behind Every activity is restartable and untimed; early finishers are asked to explain the concept to their group, which keeps them working
Students worried about being graded Anxiety reduces the willingness to experiment, which is the whole point of the session States clearly at the start that nothing is graded; feedback is phrased around the program being wrong, never the student
Session runs over time The post-test would be rushed, weakening the measurement The post-test and feedback forms are kept short enough to complete inside fifteen minutes; the core phase is the only part that may be trimmed
Language barrier in technical terms Words like "variable" and "loop" may be unfamiliar in any language Every term is introduced only after its activity, with an example the students have just experienced, and rephrased in plain language
Photographs not permitted Documentation expects visual evidence Permission sought in advance; where refused, the report uses diagrams and the signed attendance record instead of images

Section 8

Recommendations

What should be done differently, and what should be done next.

For the next session

  1. Add a follow-up visit. A second visit two to three weeks later, repeating only the post-test, would show whether the learning lasted — which the current design cannot prove.
  2. Use a comparison group. Running the same test with a class that does not receive the session would separate the effect of the activities from the effect of the visit itself.
  3. Collect the hardest concept explicitly. Ask the teacher which concept the students still seemed unsure about, and record it, so the finding can be shared.
  4. Plan for one computer per two students where possible, and confirm the number of working devices with the school during the pre-visit.
  5. Extend the core phase rather than cutting it if the session is short of time.

For the school

  1. Repeat the activities with the next batch of students — the resource needs no training to run.
  2. Use the activities as an introduction before formal programming lessons, so that the vocabulary arrives after the experience.
  3. Keep a printed copy of the survey instruments, so the exercise can be run even without devices.
  4. Encourage group work rather than one student per device — the discussion is where most of the learning happens.

For the resource itself

  1. Add more levels to the Algorithm Maze for students who finish quickly.
  2. Provide a printable paper version of every activity for schools without devices.
  3. Support a second language for the instructions, so terms are explained in the students' own language before the English term is given.
  4. Add a teacher view that shows which questions the class found hardest, using anonymous counts.

For the CEP process

  1. Fix the field session date early enough to allow a pre-visit, so the need assessment is based on the actual school rather than on general assumptions.
  2. Obtain written permission for photographs before the visit, not on the day.
  3. Enter all real data into the placeholders in the same week as the visit, while the details are still accurate.
  4. Keep the survey responses in a single numbered file, matched to the pre-test by an anonymous code rather than by name, so individuals cannot be identified.

Section 9

Conclusion

This project set out to test a simple proposition: that beginners struggle with programming not because the ideas are too difficult, but because the ideas are usually presented in the wrong order. By placing the experience of sequence, debugging, conditions, repetition and variables before their names, the five activities in this project give students something to succeed at first and a vocabulary second.

The result is a complete, offline-capable learning resource that a school can keep and reuse, together with the instruments needed to measure whether it works. The measurement design is deliberately modest about what it can prove: it shows whether understanding improved across a single session, and it does not pretend to show that the learning persisted or that it would generalise to another classroom. Those limits are stated openly because a project that overstates its evidence is worth less than one that reports honestly what it found.

What the project demonstrates beyond doubt is that the barrier beginners face is pedagogical rather than intellectual — and that it can be lowered with a maze, a bug hunt and a pattern puzzle. That is a finding small enough to be true and useful enough to be acted on, which is precisely what the Community Engagement Project asks of its students.

Status of the results in this prototype

This is a one-day/one-night prototype. The activities, instruments and analysis format are complete and functional. The Dashboard shows how the findings will be presented, using clearly-labelled sample figures. Real results must be entered after an actual field visit, in the placeholders marked throughout this site.
